# Frameworks the council uses

These are widely taught lenses from design-strategy and strategy-consulting practice, used here as working tools rather than rituals. Each section says what it is and exactly where the council applies it.

## Desirable · feasible · viable
An idea needs all three: people want it (desirable), this founder can build it in their budget (feasible), and the economics work (viable). The rubric's criteria map to these: pain, switch intent, craft → desirability; shippability, support burden → feasibility; money, reach, distribution → viability. An idea strong on two but dead on the third dies — don't average it away. *Applied in:* rubric design and chair rulings.

## Jobs-to-be-done
People "hire" a product to make progress in a situation. Miners capture each complaint as:

> When **[situation]**, I want to **[motivation]**, so I can **[desired outcome]** — but today I **[workaround]**.

Also tag the job type: **functional** (get a thing done), **emotional** (feel confident, calm, in control), **social** (look competent to someone). Many "feature" requests are really emotional jobs.

**Four forces of switching** map directly onto mined fields: *push* (the complaint), *pull* (the wedge), *anxiety* (switch cost, data lock-in, risk), *habit* (the workaround they've settled into). An idea needs push + pull to beat anxiety + habit; the skeptic probes the second pair. *Applied in:* miners, synthesis, customer advocate, skeptic.

## Opportunity score
When evidence supports it, rank jobs by **importance + max(importance − satisfaction, 0)** on a 1–10 scale — important jobs that current solutions serve badly score highest. From public evidence, estimate importance from severity × independent voices and satisfaction from how people describe the incumbent. Mark it an estimate. When evidence can't support these scales, rank by *independent voices × money present × switch intent* instead. *Applied in:* synthesis.

## Kano
Features are **must-be** (absence kills it, presence earns nothing), **performance** (more is better, linearly) or **delighters** (unexpected, loved). A wedge is rarely a must-be; the taste editor looks for the one delighter or performance gap that justifies switching, and for must-bes that quietly inflate v1 (e.g. data import). *Applied in:* taste editor, builder.

## Double diamond
Diverge then converge, twice: first on the **problem** (territories → evidence → synthesis picks what to solve), then on the **solution** (ten lenses → gauntlet picks what to build). The council is two diamonds in sequence; don't let ideation start before the problem diamond has closed. *Applied in:* pipeline stages 1–7.

## Issue trees & hypothesis-driven work
Start from the governing question and break it into **MECE** branches (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive): e.g. *Is there money?* / *Is there pain?* / *Can this founder win?* / *Why now?* Write an initial hypothesis for each branch, then gather evidence designed to confirm or kill it. This keeps scouting purposeful and makes it obvious when the evidence has answered a branch. Check MECE by asking "what would belong in none of these branches?" and "what belongs in two?" *Applied in:* stage 1 and the hypothesis scorecard in synthesis.

## Answer-first communication
Lead with the recommendation, then three supporting reasons, then risks and the ask — the pyramid principle. Busy readers should get the decision in 90 seconds and the proof on demand. *Applied in:* `council-memo.md`.

## Sizing with ranges
Bottom-up beats top-down for a solo founder: **reachable buyers × price × expected penetration**, each with low/base/high and the assumption written next to it.

- Required customers = target MRR ÷ price.
- Required penetration = required customers ÷ reachable buyers.
- If required penetration exceeds roughly 3–5% of the *reachable* pool within a year, flag it: either the pool is too small or the money floor is too high.

State what the pool is made of (e.g. "members of three communities totalling ~40k"), not an abstract TAM. *Applied in:* synthesis, economist.

## Unit economics
- **Gross margin** per customer after payments and infrastructure.
- **Churn:** estimate monthly churn from the segment and label it an assumption.
- **LTV ≈ ARPU × gross margin ÷ monthly churn.**
- **CAC payback** = acquisition cost ÷ (ARPU × gross margin). For a cold-start solo founder with no budget, CAC is mostly time — price the hours.
- Flat low prices need low support: one support ticket per customer per month can erase a $15 margin. *Applied in:* economist, risk reviewer.

## Competitive forces & copy-time
A short version of the five-forces check: **rivalry** (how many incumbents, how entrenched), **substitutes** (spreadsheets, a freelancer, doing nothing), **buyer power** (can they walk away free?), **supplier/platform power** (do you depend on an API or app store that can change terms?), **entrants** (how easy is it for others to copy). Then ask the sharpest question: **how long would the strongest incumbent need to match the wedge, and would they bother?** If the answer is "a week and yes", it's not a wedge. *Applied in:* incumbent strategist.

## Elements of value
A lens for *what kind of value* the wedge delivers: saves time, cuts cost, reduces effort or anxiety, organises, connects, provides access, gives a sense of control or pride. Naming it forces specificity — "saves time" is vague; "removes the 40-minute weekly reconciliation" is a wedge. A product that delivers only one low-order value (a small discount) is fragile. *Applied in:* customer advocate, taste editor.

## Three horizons
Classify each finalist as **H1** (extends something working now — fastest revenue), **H2** (an emerging opportunity worth testing), or **H3** (a longer, bigger bet). The runner-up the chair parks should usually be in a *different* horizon from the winner, so the founder has a considered fallback rather than a near-duplicate. *Applied in:* chair.

## Eliminate · reduce · raise · create
From blue-ocean strategy: against the incumbent, what do we **eliminate** (features everyone assumes), **reduce** (below standard), **raise** (above standard), **create** (new)? A credible wedge usually eliminates more than it creates. Every pitch states its ERRC line. *Applied in:* ideators, taste editor.

## Journey and service blueprint
For each finalist, map the customer journey — *discover → sign up → first value → pay → return* — and beneath it the **blueprint**: what the product does, what the founder does manually, what third-party systems are touched. It exposes hidden complexity (email deliverability, e-signature, imports) and finds the **moment of truth**: the one step that, if it feels great, makes the product. Time-box the first value: can a stranger get it in the first 60 seconds? *Applied in:* design strategist, builder.

## Premortem
Assume it is twelve months from now and the product failed. Write the story of why — five concrete causes, each tied to something visible in the evidence or the brief today. Rank by likelihood × impact; the top two become red-team targets and assumption-register entries. A premortem is more candid than a risk list because failure is already assumed. *Applied in:* premortem role, red team, assumptions register.

## The wedge test
A wedge is the **single reason** a specific customer would switch *now*. It passes if all hold:
1. It is stated in one sentence naming the customer and the change in their situation.
2. It isn't just a lower price (undercutting by a few dollars is not a wedge; the incumbent can match it).
3. The incumbent is unlikely or unable to copy it quickly.
4. It's visible in the evidence — people are already complaining about exactly this.
5. It can be demonstrated in the first minute of use.

## Bias checklist
Before the chair rules, say which of these could be distorting the run:

- **Loud minority** — a few angry posters aren't a market. Check independent voices and source families.
- **Survivorship** — reviews come from people who stayed long enough to complain; silent churners are invisible.
- **Recency** — a viral thread or this month's price hike may overweight a temporary mood.
- **Confirmation** — scouts searching only for phrases that support a favourite territory.
- **Founder-fit bias** — preferring ideas that flatter the founder's skills over ones with the best evidence (or the reverse: dismissing a strong idea as "not me").
- **Anchoring** — the first idea pitched shapes the rest. Randomise judging order and score blind.
- **Groupthink** — judges who saw each other's scores converge. Keep scoring independent.
- **Narrative fallacy** — a compelling pitch outweighing thin evidence. The scoreboard, not the story, decides.
